SEP IRA vs Solo 401(k) for Freelancers (2026): Which One Lets You Save More?

By LedgerLane · November 28, 2026

Daniel is a freelance web developer in Denver. Last year he netted just over $100,000 from client work, paid his quarterly taxes on time, and still watched a five-figure tax bill eat the money he had meant to save. This year he wants the saving to happen before the tax bill does — and he has narrowed it to two accounts every self-employed guide names: a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k). Both are built for exactly his situation. Both shelter money from tax. But at his income, one of them lets him put away more than twice as much as the other.

The choice matters because freelancers get no employer match, no automatic payroll deduction, and no HR department nudging them to enroll. The account you pick is the whole retirement plan. Pick the simple one and you may leave $24,500 a year on the table. Pick the powerful one and you take on a December deadline and a form you cannot forget. This guide runs the two head to head on the numbers that decide it: how much each lets you contribute in 2026, when the money has to be in, what happens if you hire help, and which one fits which freelancer.

The short answer: For most freelancers, the solo 401(k) wins on size. In 2026 it allows a $24,500 employee deferral plus an employer contribution of up to 25% of compensation, capped at $72,000 in total — while a SEP IRA allows only the employer half, capped at the lesser of 25% of compensation or $72,000. At $100,000 of net earnings that is $44,500 in a solo 401(k) versus $20,000 in a SEP. The SEP wins only on simplicity: no December deadline, no annual filing, nothing to set up in advance.

The 2026 limits, side by side

The IRS sets both plans' limits in the same annual notice — Notice 2025-67 for 2026 — and the 2026 numbers moved up from 2025, so guides quoting last year's figures will short-change you. Here is the comparison that governs everything below:

Employee deferral Not allowed — employer contributions only Up to $24,500
Employer contribution Up to 25% of compensation Up to 25% of compensation
Total cap, under 50 Lesser of 25% or $72,000 $72,000 (employee + employer)
Catch-up, age 50–59 and 64+ None +$8,000 (total $80,000)
Catch-up, age 60–63 None +$11,250 (total $83,250)

One number in that table needs a warning before the examples make sense. The "25% of compensation" is not 25% of your Schedule C profit. For a sole proprietor, the IRS first subtracts half your self-employment tax, and then the contribution itself comes out of the figure it is calculated on — Publication 560's worksheet turns the headline 25% into an effective rate of about 20% of your net earnings. Every worked example below uses 20%, not 25%, because 25% of gross profit is the single most common overstatement in freelance retirement guides.

Round 1: How much can you actually put in?

This is the round the solo 401(k) wins, and it is not close at ordinary freelance incomes — because the solo 401(k) has two halves and the SEP has one.

At $60,000 of net earnings. A SEP IRA takes 20% of $60,000: $12,000. A solo 401(k) takes the same $12,000 employer half plus the full $24,500 employee deferral: $36,500. The solo 401(k) shelters three times as much.

At $100,000 of net earnings. A SEP IRA takes 20% of $100,000: $20,000. A solo 401(k) takes $24,500 + $20,000: $44,500. The gap is $24,500 — exactly the employee deferral, and it stays exactly that size at every income below the caps.

At $300,000 of net earnings. A SEP IRA takes 20% of $300,000: $60,000. A solo 401(k) takes $24,500 + $60,000 = $84,500, but the $72,000 annual-additions cap cuts it back to $72,000. The gap narrows to $12,000 — both plans max out at $72,000; the solo 401(k) just gets there at a much lower income.

At age 50 or older, the gap widens again. The SEP has no catch-up contribution at any age. The solo 401(k) adds $8,000 at ages 50–59 (and 64+), and $11,250 at ages 60–63. A 55-year-old freelancer netting $100,000 can put $52,500 in a solo 401(k) against $20,000 in a SEP.

The honest limitation on the solo 401(k)'s side: the $24,500 employee deferral is a single ceiling shared across every 401(k) you participate in during the year. If you also have a part-time job with a 401(k) and defer $10,000 there, only $14,500 of deferral room is left for the solo plan. The employer half is separate for each unrelated business, so the solo plan stays worthwhile for side income — but the headline $24,500 assumes the solo plan is your only 401(k).

Round 2: The deadlines — where the SEP fights back

The SEP IRA's best feature is that it has almost no deadline pressure. You can open one and fund it for 2026 as late as your tax-filing deadline, including extensions — April 15, 2027 for most sole proprietors, or October 15, 2027 if you extend. There is no plan to adopt in advance and no election to make in December.

The solo 401(k) is stricter, and this is the round it can lose. To make an employee deferral for 2026, the plan must be established and the deferral election must be in writing by December 31, 2026 — the money itself can be deposited later, up to your filing deadline, but the decision cannot. Miss December 31 and the $24,500 half of the plan is gone for the year. The employer half is more forgiving: it can be deposited up to the filing deadline, like a SEP contribution.

Round 3: Roth, loans and the features that only one plan has

Roth. A solo 401(k) can accept Roth elective deferrals — after-tax money now, tax-free withdrawals later — if the plan document offers the option, and most major providers' solo plans do. A SEP IRA cannot: every SEP contribution is pre-tax, and the only route to Roth treatment is converting the money to a Roth IRA afterwards, which is a taxable event in the year you convert. For a freelancer in a low-income year — a slow year, a sabbatical, the first year of the business — the Roth deferral is a lever the SEP simply does not have.

Loans. Many solo 401(k) plans allow you to borrow from your own balance, generally up to 50% of the account or $50,000, whichever is less, if the plan document permits it. A SEP IRA never allows loans. A loan is not a reason to choose a plan — borrowing from retirement savings should be a last resort — but in a genuine emergency the option exists in one plan and not the other.

Hiring help. This is the SEP's hidden cost and the solo 401(k)'s hard limit. A SEP plan must cover every eligible employee at the same percentage you give yourself — generally anyone 21 or older who worked for you in three of the last five years and earned at least $800 in 2026. Give yourself 20% and you owe your assistant 20% too. A solo 401(k), by definition, covers a business owner with no employees other than a spouse. Hire your first employee and the solo 401(k) stops being available for future contributions. So the SEP suits the freelancer who is about to hire; the solo 401(k) suits the freelancer who will stay solo.

Round 4: Paperwork, cost and the form you cannot forget

The SEP IRA is the simplest retirement account in the American tax code: no annual filing, no plan document to maintain, no deadline to track. You open the account, contribute, and deduct. Setup takes an afternoon and costs nothing at most brokerages.

The solo 401(k) asks for a little more. You sign a plan adoption agreement, get a plan EIN, and — the one obligation that catches people out — once the plan's combined assets pass $250,000, you must file Form 5500-EZ with the IRS every year, due July 31. The form is short, but the penalty for forgetting it is not. Set a calendar reminder for the July after your balance crosses $250,000, and the obligation is a five-minute annual chore rather than a trap.

Neither plan charges the account itself a fee worth worrying about at the big brokerages — the cost of the solo 401(k) is attention, not money. If you are the kind of person who files quarterly estimated taxes on time — our guide to quarterly estimated taxes for freelancers covers the dates and safe-harbor rules — the solo 401(k)'s extra paperwork will not trouble you. If you are not, the SEP's zero-maintenance design is a feature, not a compromise.

The verdict

Freelancer Pick
Solo, earning $60,000–$300,000, wants the biggest shelter Solo 401(k) — the $24,500 deferral is free money the SEP cannot match
Age 50+, catching up on retirement Solo 401(k) — the catch-up alone is worth up to $11,250 a year
Wants a Roth option or a loan backstop Solo 401(k) — the SEP offers neither
About to hire an employee SEP IRA — it covers staff at the same rate; the solo 401(k) closes when you hire
Missed December 31, or wants zero paperwork SEP IRA — fundable until your filing deadline, no annual form
Income under ~$30,000 Either — the deferral still helps in a solo 401(k), but the difference is small and simplicity may win

The one mistake to avoid is choosing neither. A freelancer with no retirement account pays tax on every dollar and saves whatever is left — which, in a good year, is usually less than the plan would have sheltered and, in a bad year, is nothing. Open one of them this week: the solo 401(k) before December 31 if you want the bigger number, the SEP any time before you file if you want the simpler one. Frequently asked questions

Can I have both a SEP IRA and a solo 401(k)?

Yes, you can hold both — but the employer contributions across both plans share the same $72,000 annual-additions limit for 2026, so holding both does not double your room. The usual reason to hold both is timing: a SEP opened in March for last year's income alongside a solo 401(k) already running for this year. For most freelancers, one plan, funded properly, beats two plans funded halfway.

How much can I put in a solo 401(k) if I earn $100,000?

With $100,000 of net self-employment earnings in 2026: a $24,500 employee deferral plus an employer contribution of about 20% of net earnings — $20,000 — for a total of $44,500. At 50 or older, add the $8,000 catch-up for $52,500. The same $100,000 in a SEP IRA allows $20,000.

Is a SEP IRA better than a solo 401(k) for a freelancer?

The solo 401(k) is better on contribution size, catch-up contributions, the Roth option and loan access. The SEP IRA is better on simplicity, deadlines and hiring — it can be opened and funded until your tax-filing deadline, needs no annual filing, and covers employees at the same rate. Solo freelancers who want the maximum shelter should pick the solo 401(k); freelancers who will hire, or who missed December 31, should pick the SEP.

What is the deadline for a solo 401(k) contribution for 2026?

The plan must be established and your employee deferral elected in writing by December 31, 2026. The money itself, and the employer contribution, can be deposited up to your tax-filing deadline including extensions. A SEP IRA for 2026 can be opened and funded entirely by that filing deadline — April 15, 2027, or October 15, 2027 with an extension.

Does a SEP IRA have catch-up contributions?

No. The IRS does not allow catch-up contributions in SEP plans at any age — the 2026 limit is the lesser of 25% of compensation or $72,000, and that is the ceiling. A solo 401(k) allows an $8,000 catch-up at ages 50–59 and 64+, and $11,250 at ages 60–63.

The bottom line

At $100,000 of net freelance earnings in 2026, the solo 401(k) shelters $44,500 and the SEP IRA shelters $20,000 — the same income, the same tax year, a $24,500 difference that comes entirely from the employee deferral the SEP does not have. The SEP's answer is real too: no December deadline, no annual form, and room for employees when you hire. Choose the solo 401(k) if you are staying solo and can sign the papers before December 31; choose the SEP if you are hiring, missed the deadline, or want retirement saving to need no attention at all. Either way, choose one — the most expensive retirement plan for a freelancer is the one that was never opened.
