How to Set Your Freelance Rates in 2026: A Step-by-Step Guide

By LedgerLane · November 27, 2026

Maya is a freelance brand designer in Austin. She left a staff job paying $62,000 a year, set her freelance rate at $35 an hour — roughly her old salary divided by 2,080 hours — and felt good about it. Six months later she was working longer hours than at the agency, and a $4,200 tax bill arrived that she had not saved for. The problem was that $35 an hour was never a real rate at all — a salary figure with no room for taxes, no room for the hours she could not bill, and no room for the software, insurance and dry months of self-employment.

Most freelancers set their first rate the same way Maya did: copy a number from a forum, divide an old salary by 2,080, or pick something low enough to win the job. All three lose money the same quiet way — a little every week, until the year ends short. This guide does it the other way round: start with what the market actually pays, work out the lowest rate your own numbers allow, pick a pricing model, then test the rate and raise it on a schedule.

The short answer: First find your market range — the median US freelancer advertises $65 an hour on Upwork in 2026, though the global median is $30. Then work out your floor with this formula: (the income you need + your business costs) ÷ your billable hours, then divide by 0.75 to leave room for roughly 25% in taxes. A freelancer who needs $75,000 a year, spends $5,000 on the business and bills 1,200 hours lands at about $89 an hour. Charge at or above that floor, never below it.

Step 1: Start with the market, not your gut

Your rate is not a statement about your worth. It is a price in a market, and the market publishes its numbers. The most useful benchmark this month comes from Upwork Insights, which read the public hourly rates on 47,795 Upwork freelancer profiles with at least $10,000 in lifetime earnings. Its 2026 report, with data as of November 26, 2026, puts the median advertised rate at $30 an hour worldwide — and $65 an hour for freelancers in the United States. The US median is more than double the global one, because most of the platform's freelancers live in lower-cost countries; if you are a US freelancer, $65 is your comparison, not $30. (Upwork Insights is independent and is not affiliated with Upwork Inc.)

The best-paid skills tell the same story: Startup Consultants advertise a median of $80 an hour, Ad Copy Professionals and Consultants $60, Fractional CFOs $55. The lowest-paid — Prospect Lists at $7, Data Collection at $8 — are the ones a client can replace with a tool. Rates follow how hard the skill is to replace, not how hard the work feels.

Government salary data gives the other side — what the same work pays as a job. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' May 2024 wage survey puts the median annual wage for web developers at $90,930 and for web and digital interface designers at $98,090. Spread over a standard 2,080-hour work year, that is about $43.71 and $47.16 an hour. Two warnings: BLS medians cover employees, not the self-employed, and an employee's wage comes with paid holidays, health insurance and an employer paying half the payroll tax — none of which a freelancer gets. A freelancer's rate has to sit above the equivalent salary's hourly figure, not equal to it.

Step 2: Work out your floor with the formula

A market range tells you what other people charge. Your floor tells you what you must charge — the rate below which the year does not add up:

(Income you need + business costs) ÷ billable hours ÷ 0.75 = your floor rate

The income you need is the salary you want to live on. Business costs are the real ones: software, insurance, a laptop fund, bank fees — $5,000 a year is modest for most freelancers. Billable hours are not 2,080: a 40-hour week holds perhaps 25 billable hours once you subtract finding clients, admin and invoicing, which is 1,200 hours over 48 working weeks. The 0.75 leaves room for roughly 25% of gross going to taxes — self-employment tax alone is 15.3%, before income tax.

Worked example one. Maya wants $75,000 a year to live on and spends $5,000 running the business. That is $80,000 ÷ 1,200 billable hours = $66.67 an hour before tax. Divide by 0.75 and the floor is $88.89 — call it $89 an hour. Check it backwards: $89 × 1,200 is $106,800 gross; set aside 25% for tax ($26,700) and $80,100 remains — the $75,000 she needs plus the $5,000 the business costs. At her old $35 an hour, the same 1,200 hours would have grossed $42,000, keeping about $31,500 after tax — less than half of what she needed.

Worked example two. A newer freelancer, aiming for $50,000 a year with $3,000 of business costs and 1,000 billable hours: $53,000 ÷ 1,000 = $53, then ÷ 0.75 = $70.67, so about $71 an hour. That checks out: $71 × 1,000 = $71,000 gross, minus 25% tax leaves $53,250 — just enough. Notice what the formula does to beginners: even a modest $50,000 target needs $71 an hour once taxes and non-billable time are counted — which is why "start cheap and raise it later" so often ends in a year of working full-time for part-time pay.

Step 3: Pick the pricing model that fits the work

The floor rate is an hourly number, but you do not have to sell hours. Three models dominate freelance work:

  • Hourly. Best when the scope is open-ended — maintenance, retainers, ongoing support. The floor rate applies directly. The risk: getting faster punishes you, since finishing in half the time earns half the money.
  • Project (flat) fee. Best when the deliverable is clear — a logo set, a landing page, a month of social posts. Price it as your floor rate × the hours you expect, plus a margin for revisions. The client buys certainty; you keep the upside when you work efficiently. Always state how many revision rounds are included.
  • Value-based. Best when your work moves a number the client cares about — a checkout page, a sales deck, a campaign. The price follows the value created, not the hours spent, and it can sit far above the floor. It only works when you can name the value in the client's own terms and the client is big enough for the number to matter. For most early-career freelancers, project pricing with a floor underneath is the honest middle ground.

Whichever model you choose, the floor still governs: a project fee that works out below your floor rate is a loss dressed as a win. Run the division before you quote.

Step 4: Adjust for platform fees and taxes

If the work comes through a platform, the platform takes its cut before you see a cent. Upwork's freelancer service fee is now variable — 0% to 15% per contract, set when the contract starts and fixed for its life, according to Upwork's own support page. At the common 10% level, a $65-an-hour contract pays you $58.50 — so if your floor is $65, quote about $72 on the platform to keep $65. Fiverr takes a flat 20% of every order, so the same logic applies harder there. Direct clients cost you 0% — one reason the same freelancer can charge less off-platform and still keep more.

Taxes need the same treatment. As a freelancer you pay self-employment tax of 15.3% on your net earnings, plus income tax — and nobody withholds either for you. Our guide to quarterly estimated taxes for freelancers covers the payment dates and safe-harbor rules; move 25–30% of every payment into a separate account the day it lands. The separate account matters more than the percentage: money in your main balance gets spent, and the tax bill does not negotiate. Our guide to the best bank accounts for freelancers covers where that second account should live.

Step 5: Test the rate, then raise it on a schedule

A rate is a hypothesis until a client accepts it. Test yours on the next three quotes: if all three accept immediately, you are probably too cheap; if all three walk away, the rate may be ahead of your proof — portfolio, testimonials, niche. One or two acceptances out of three is about right. And never quote a rate you have not said out loud first.

Then raise it on a schedule, not on a whim. Review every six months, and raise 10–15% when you are booked two to three weeks out, when clients accept without negotiating, or when your skills have moved up a level. Tell existing clients first, in writing, with 30 days' notice and a reason tied to the work. Grandfathering a loyal early client at the old rate for one project is generosity; doing it for a year is a pay cut you chose.

The mistakes that quietly cost freelancers thousands

  • Copying a competitor's rate. Their costs, clients and experience are not yours — the formula in Step 2 is the only rate that knows your numbers.
  • Forgetting the hours you cannot bill. Admin, pitching, invoicing — roughly 15 of every 40 hours. A rate built on 2,080 billable hours is built on a year that does not exist.
  • Quoting before counting revisions and meetings. A "quick call" is billable time, and a third revision round is a scope change — put both in the quote.
  • Letting a platform fee eat the floor. A 10% fee on a $65 rate is $6.50 an hour gone. Price the fee in, or take the client direct.
  • Never raising the rate. Inflation, new skills and a fuller book all push the market up — a rate unchanged for two years has fallen behind.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a beginner freelancer charge per hour?

Work out your floor with the formula above first — for a $50,000 income target with modest costs, that lands near $71 an hour. Then compare it with the market: the median US freelancer advertises $65 an hour on Upwork in 2026, and beginners in common skills often start in the $25–$50 range while they build proof. If your floor is above what the market will pay today, close the gap by building proof rather than by working below your floor for a year.

What is a good freelance hourly rate in 2026?

For US freelancers, the honest range is roughly $50–$100 an hour for established skills, with a $65 median on Upwork. Scarce skills command more — startup consultants advertise an $80 median, ad copy professionals $60. The rate that matters is yours: the floor from your own income, costs and billable hours, checked against the market for your skill.

Should I charge hourly or per project?

Hourly when the scope is open or the client keeps adding work; per project when the deliverable is clear and you can estimate the hours. Project pricing usually earns more for experienced freelancers, because faster work keeps the fee. Either way, run the floor-rate division first.

How do I calculate my freelance rate from my old salary?

Do not divide your salary by 2,080. Use (income needed + business costs) ÷ billable hours (about 1,200 a year) ÷ 0.75 for taxes. A $75,000 target with $5,000 of costs lands at about $89 an hour — well above the $36 the salary-divided-by-2,080 shortcut gives, and the difference is exactly the taxes, costs and unbillable hours it ignores.

When should I raise my freelance rates?

Every six months, by 10–15%, when you are booked two to three weeks out, when clients accept without negotiating, or when your skills have moved up a level. Give existing clients 30 days' written notice. If every quote is accepted instantly, you are overdue a raise.

The bottom line

Your rate is three numbers, not one: the market range, your floor, and the price you actually quote — and the quote must never fall below the floor. Start with the benchmarks ($65 an hour is the US median in 2026), run the formula with your own income, costs and 1,200 billable hours, add room for platform fees and taxes, then test the number on three real quotes and raise it every six months. Maya's $35 an hour was not a rate; it was a hope. A rate you can defend, with arithmetic behind it, is what turns freelancing from a hustle into a living — and once the rate is set, the hours still have to become money, which is why our guide on how to invoice as a freelancer is the natural next read.
