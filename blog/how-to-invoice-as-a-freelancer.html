How to Invoice as a Freelancer in the US (2026): 6 Steps to Getting Paid on Time

By LedgerLane · November 23, 2026

Maya is a freelance brand designer in Austin. In September she finished a $2,400 logo package for a local café — on time, on brief, and the owner loved it. She sent the invoice nine days later, because the invoice felt like admin. It had no due date, no late fee, and no payment link — just her bank details in an email.

The café paid 47 days later. Not because the owner was difficult. Because nothing in that invoice told him when to pay, what would happen if he didn't, or made paying easy. Maya spent six weeks checking her bank balance every morning for money she had already earned.

That is the real lesson about invoicing. It is not paperwork. It is the difference between doing the work and getting paid for it. A good invoice is short, specific, sent fast, and hard to ignore. Here is how to write one, step by step.

The short answer: Agree the rate, deposit and due date before you start, then invoice the day the work is delivered — with your business details, an invoice number, an itemised list, the total, a due date and a payment link. Net 15 terms plus a 50% deposit get most freelancers paid faster than Net 30 with no deposit.

Step 1: Agree the money terms before you start

The invoice is not where payment terms are decided — it is where they are written down. If the rate, deposit and due date are not agreed before the work begins, you will negotiate them after the work is done, when your leverage is gone. Before you start, get these four things in writing — an email counts:

  • The rate and what it covers. Hourly or fixed, and exactly what is included: "Logo package, $2,400, includes three concepts and two revision rounds" leaves nothing to argue about later.
  • A deposit. For project work, 50% upfront is the standard. It filters out clients who were never going to pay, and you are never more than half an invoice away from whole.
  • The due date. Pick it now, not at invoicing time. Net 15 (15 days after the invoice date) is a good default — shorter than the Net 30 big companies prefer, and short enough that the work is still fresh in the client's mind.
  • A late fee. There is no single federal rule on late fees — what you can charge depends on your state and, most importantly, on what your contract says. Put it in the contract: for example, 1.5% per month on overdue balances.

One more thing to decide now: where the money lands. Getting paid into a separate business account keeps your freelance income clean for tax time. If you have not set one up yet, our guide to the best bank account for freelancers compares six free and low-cost options.

Step 2: Put these nine things on every invoice

A missing detail is a legitimate reason to delay. "I didn't know where to send it" and "I wasn't sure what this was for" are the two most common excuses, and both are preventable. Every invoice should carry:

  1. Your business name and contact details — the name you trade under, your email and phone.
  2. The client's name and address — the person or company who hired you, not just a first name.
  3. A unique invoice number — INV-1042, or 2026-011. Sequential numbers stop the same invoice being paid twice.
  4. The invoice date and the due date — both, in full. "Due November 30, 2026" beats "due in 15 days".
  5. An itemised list of the work — what you did, how many hours or units, and the rate for each line. "Design services — 4 hours × $75" is clear; "Services — $300" invites questions.
  6. The total, with any tax shown separately — most US freelancers do not charge sales tax on services, but if your state requires it, show it as its own line.
  7. Your payment details or a payment link — a clickable "Pay now" button beats bank details pasted in an email. Fewer steps between reading and paying means faster payment.
  8. The payment terms in one line — "Payment due within 15 days. A 1.5% monthly late fee applies after the due date."
  9. A one-line thank-you — it costs nothing and reads like a professional, not a debt collector.

Keep a copy of every invoice in the same place, with the payment record attached. At tax time you will need them, and if a client ever disputes a payment, the invoice is your evidence. Keeping your business money in its own account helps here too — it is one of the reasons our guide to business checking vs personal checking recommends separating them from day one.

Step 3: Send it the day the work lands

This is the step most freelancers get wrong, and it costs the most money. Every day between delivery and invoicing is a day the client forgets the work or spends the budget elsewhere.

The rule is simple: invoice the same day you deliver. Not on Friday, not at the end of the month. The work is fresh, the client is happiest, and the payment clock starts while the value is obvious. For ongoing work, set a rhythm and keep it — weekly for hourly work, monthly for retainers, on delivery for fixed projects. Recurring invoices in the tools below go out automatically, so you stop being the person who has to remember.

Step 4: Set payment terms that protect you

Payment terms are a negotiation, and freelancers give away more here than anywhere else. Three choices matter:

  • Net 15 beats Net 30. Big companies ask for Net 30 because their accounts departments run monthly. Small clients rarely need it, and most accept Net 15 without a blink if it is in the agreement from the start.
  • Due on receipt for small jobs. For anything under a few hundred dollars, "due on receipt" is fair and normal. Nobody needs 30 days to pay $250.
  • Milestones for big projects. A $6,000 project should never be one invoice at the end. Split it: 50% to start, 25% at the midpoint, 25% on delivery. If a client disappears halfway, you have been paid for the work you did.

And the deposit, once more, because it matters that much: a client who pays 50% upfront has already decided you are worth paying. The freelancers who get burned worst are almost always the ones who started work on a promise.

Step 5: Pick a tool that fits how you bill

You can invoice from a spreadsheet — for the first few clients, it works. The moment you have more than a handful of invoices a month, a tool pays for itself in automatic reminders, payment links, and never losing track of who owes what.

The honest way to choose is by how the tool charges you. Three of the four below cost nothing per month and earn their money from card fees when a client pays; FreshBooks charges a subscription instead. That changes the maths completely depending on how much you bill.

Zoho Invoice is the cheapest way to invoice, full stop. FreshBooks is the invoicing-first tool: Lite at $23 a month covers 5 billable clients with estimates, recurring billing and time tracking; Plus at $43 raises the cap to 50 and adds retainers. Step 6: Follow up like a professional, not a beggar

A late invoice is rarely personal — usually a busy person, a lost email, or an accounts department on its own schedule. The freelancers who get paid fastest follow up early, politely, and on a schedule.

  • On the due date: a short note — "Hi, just a reminder that invoice INV-1042 for $1,200 is due today. Payment link here." No apology, no explanation.
  • Three days late: the same note, once more. Assume it was missed, not refused.
  • Seven days late: a firmer note, with the late fee mentioned: "This is now 7 days overdue; the 1.5% monthly late fee in our agreement applies from the due date."
  • Fourteen days late: a phone call, then a written final notice. And pause the work — never keep delivering to a client who has not paid for the last delivery.

Two things make this easier: automatic reminders, which most of the tools above send for you, and a paper trail — every follow-up in writing, every payment recorded. If a client ever does not pay at all, that trail is what a small-claims filing is built on, and clean records are also what builds your business credit profile over time.

The bottom line

Invoicing well is a habit, not a talent: agree the terms before you start, take a deposit, send the invoice the day the work lands, give it a due date that respects your time, and follow up on a schedule. The tool matters less than the habit — Wave, Zoho Invoice and Square Invoices all cost nothing a month to start, and FreshBooks at $23 a month is the pick if you bill by the hour. The expensive mistake is the invoice you never send.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a business bank account to send an invoice?

No — you can invoice as a sole proprietor using your own name and a personal account. But a separate business account makes tax time far simpler and looks more professional. Our guide to the best bank account for freelancers compares six free and low-cost options.

Can I charge a late fee on an overdue invoice?

Yes, if it was agreed in advance. There is no single federal late-fee rule in the US — what you can charge depends on your state and on your contract. Put the fee in your written agreement before the work starts and repeat it on the invoice.

How soon should I send an invoice after finishing work?

The same day. Every day you wait is a day the client forgets the work or spends the budget elsewhere. Same-day invoicing is the single easiest way to get paid faster, and recurring invoices in Wave, Zoho Invoice or FreshBooks go out automatically for repeat work.
