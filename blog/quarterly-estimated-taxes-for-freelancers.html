Quarterly Estimated Taxes for Freelancers: 2026 Dates, Safe Harbor Rules and How to Pay

By LedgerLane · November 25, 2026

Maya is a freelance brand designer in Austin. Her first full year of freelancing went well — about $60,000 of profit — and she treated tax the way she had at her old job: something that gets sorted out in April. Then April came. The bill ran past $11,000 once self-employment tax was added, plus an underpayment penalty on top — because the IRS does not wait until April, and nobody had withheld a cent from her invoices.

That is the one thing every new freelancer has to learn: for the self-employed, tax is pay-as-you-go. This guide covers who has to pay, the exact 2026 due dates, the safe harbor rule that keeps you penalty-free, and a simple routine that takes about fifteen minutes, four times a year.

The quick version: Expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax for 2026? The IRS expects four payments — due April 15, June 15 and September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027. Pay the smaller of 90% of your 2026 tax or 100% of your 2025 tax (110% if 2025 income topped $150,000), and there is no penalty.

Who actually has to pay estimated tax?

The IRS rule is a threshold, not a job title. You generally have to make estimated tax payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax when you file your return, after subtracting any withholding and refundable credits. Freelancers land in this group almost by default, because no employer withholds tax from client payments — every dollar of tax is yours to send in yourself.

There is one clean exception. You do not have to pay estimated tax for 2026 if all three of these are true:

  • You had no tax liability at all in 2025
  • You were a US citizen or resident alien for the whole of 2025
  • Your 2025 tax year covered a full 12 months

That mostly describes someone who started freelancing this year after a year with no tax owed. Everyone else should assume the quarterly payments apply. Note that estimated tax also covers your self-employment tax — where most of a freelancer's bill actually comes from.

The 2026 due dates — and why they are not really "quarterly"

The four payment periods are not equal, which catches people out. The second period is only two months long, and the fourth is four months. Here is the full 2026 schedule from Form 1040-ES:

Payment Income it covers Due date
1st January 1 – March 31, 2026 April 15, 2026
2nd April 1 – May 31, 2026 June 15, 2026
3rd June 1 – August 31, 2026 September 15, 2026
4th September 1 – December 31, 2026 January 15, 2027

Three things worth knowing about these dates:

  • Weekend rule. If a due date falls on a Saturday, Sunday or federal holiday, the payment is on time if made the next business day. None of the 2026 dates needs the shift — all four fall on weekdays.
  • The January skip. You can skip the January 15, 2027 payment entirely if you file your 2026 return and pay the full balance by February 1, 2027.
  • States are separate. Most states with an income tax run their own estimated payments, often on slightly different dates. This guide is federal only — check your state's tax agency site for its schedule.

How much to pay: the safe harbor rule

You do not need to guess your 2026 tax perfectly. The IRS gives you a safe harbor: if your withholding plus estimated payments, paid on time, reach a target amount, there is no underpayment penalty — even if you end up owing more when you file. The target is the smaller of:

  • 90% of your actual 2026 tax, or
  • 100% of your total 2025 tax (the "total tax" line on last year's return) — rising to 110% if your 2025 adjusted gross income was over $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately).

For most freelancers the second number is the practical one, because it is already known. If your 2025 return showed $9,200 of total tax, your 2026 safe harbor is $9,200 ÷ 4 = $2,300 per payment — pay that on each due date and the penalty cannot touch you, whatever 2026 earns. If 2026 turns out much bigger, you still owe the difference in April: the safe harbor removes the penalty, not the tax.

The part of the bill that is not income tax

Freelancers also pay self-employment tax: 15.3% — 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare — applied to 92.35% of net profit. On $60,000 of profit, that is 0.9235 × $60,000 × 15.3% ≈ $8,478, before a single dollar of income tax. (The Social Security part stops at the 2026 wage base of $184,500; the Medicare part does not.) Half of it is deductible on your return — but this is still why the April bill shocks people who only budgeted for income tax.

A set-aside habit that works

Until your numbers settle, use the common rule of thumb: move 25–30% of every payment you receive into a separate account the day it lands. On a $4,000 month of profit, that is $1,000–$1,200 set aside, so the quarterly payment is a transfer, not a scramble. Our guide to the best bank accounts for freelancers covers accounts with free savings pockets for exactly this — and parking the set-aside in one of the best high-yield savings accounts earns it a little interest while it waits.

How to pay: the 15-minute routine, step by step

  1. Pick your target. Use the safe harbor: last year's total tax ÷ 4. If last year was not a full freelancing year, use the Form 1040-ES worksheet to estimate this year's tax instead, and take 90% of that figure ÷ 4.
  2. Check your set-aside account. The 25–30% habit above should have the money waiting. If the balance is short, pay what you can by the due date anyway — the penalty runs per period on the shortfall, so a partial on-time payment still helps.
  3. Pay the IRS. The fastest routes are free: IRS Direct Pay (a bank transfer at irs.gov — select "Estimated Tax" and Form 1040-ES for the right tax year) or your IRS online account, which also shows your payment history. The paper route still exists — a check mailed with that period's Form 1040-ES voucher — and IRS-listed processors take cards for a fee of around 2%.
  4. Save the confirmation. File the confirmation number with the tax year's records; if a payment is ever disputed, that number is your proof.
  5. Re-estimate before the next date. When income changes, refigure with a fresh 1040-ES worksheet and adjust the next payment. Clean records make this a five-minute job — one more reason to keep invoicing and expenses in order, as in our guide on how to invoice as a freelancer.

One freedom worth knowing: you do not have to pay exactly four times. The IRS accepts any rhythm — weekly, monthly — as long as enough has been paid in by each period's due date.

If your income is uneven, read this before overpaying

Freelance income rarely arrives in four equal chunks, but the standard calculation assumes it does — so equal payments can leave you "underpaid" early in the year on income you had not even earned yet.

The IRS answer is the annualized income installment method: each payment is calculated from the income actually received up to that period, and you claim it on Schedule AI of Form 2210 when you file. It takes more arithmetic (tax software handles it well), but for seasonal freelancers it can remove a penalty the flat method would wrongly charge.

What happens if you miss a payment?

The underpayment penalty works like interest: it is charged per period, on the amount underpaid, for the time it stays unpaid. Two practical consequences:

  • Pay late rather than never. Every day you bring the payment forward shrinks the charge. Do not wait for the next quarter to fix a missed one.
  • A refund does not erase it. You can owe the penalty even if you overpaid overall and get money back at filing, because the IRS tests each period separately.

Waivers exist only for narrow cases — a casualty, disaster or similar — so treat them as exceptions, not a strategy.

Frequently asked questions

This is my first year freelancing. Do I still have to pay?

Usually, yes — unless you meet all three exception conditions above. If you had a job with withholding last year and owed tax on it, the exception does not apply, and your safe harbor is based on that return's total tax.

I have a W-2 job and freelance on the side. Can I skip the quarterly payments?

Possibly. Instead of estimated payments, you can ask your employer to withhold more from your wages by filing a new Form W-4 with an extra amount on the line for additional withholding. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year even if you only increase it late — which makes it a useful fix if you discover the problem in autumn.

What if I overpay my estimated tax?

You have not lost the money. Overpayments come back as a refund when you file, or you can elect to apply them to next year's estimated tax instead. Overpaying is a cash-flow cost, not a penalty — but it is still worth re-estimating each quarter so you are not lending the IRS more than you need to.

Do I pay estimated tax on gross income or profit?

Profit. Estimated tax is figured on expected taxable income, which for a freelancer starts from net business profit — income minus ordinary business expenses — plus other income, deductions and credits. The Form 1040-ES worksheet walks through it. It is also why expense tracking matters: every legitimate expense recorded lowers the profit the tax is calculated on.

Are state estimated taxes due on the same dates?

Not always. Many states mirror the federal dates, but several use their own schedule and forms. Treat the state as a separate, smaller version of the same routine: same set-aside account, its own dates from your state's tax agency.

The bottom line

Quarterly estimated tax is not really a tax problem — it is a cash-flow habit. Know the four dates (April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15), anchor payments to the safe harbor so the penalty is off the table, set aside 25–30% of every payment the day it arrives, and spend fifteen minutes per period paying and re-estimating. Do that, and April becomes a filing exercise instead of a financial emergency.
